The physical layer is where networking stops being abstract and starts becoming real. At the end of the day, all data is transmitted as some form of electromagnetic signal, whether that is electrical signals in copper wires or light pulses in fiber. This layer matters because it sets the fundamental limits of communication. Things like how far a signal can travel, how fast data can be sent, and how much interference it faces are all determined here, not at higher layers.

One of the first decisions in the physical layer is choosing the transmission medium. Twisted pair cables are cheap and widely used for Ethernet, but they suffer from higher attenuation and shorter distance limits. Coaxial cables improve shielding and performance but are less common in modern LANs. Optical fiber stands out because it offers extremely low signal loss and massive bandwidth, allowing data to travel long distances efficiently. This is why fiber is used for backbone networks, while copper is mostly limited to shorter connections.

To understand how signals weaken over distance, we use decibels, which simplify power calculations. Instead of dealing with complicated multiplication, we can just add and subtract values. For example, if a signal starts at 30 dBm and loses 2.5 dB over a cable, the received signal becomes 27.5 dBm. This type of calculation, known as a link budget, is important because it tells us whether the signal is still strong enough to be decoded at the receiver.

Another key concept is the relationship between bandwidth and data rate. The Shannon-Hartley theorem shows that capacity increases linearly with bandwidth but only logarithmically with signal-to-noise ratio. This means increasing bandwidth is usually more effective than simply increasing power. However, increasing bandwidth also introduces more noise, which creates a tradeoff that engineers have to manage carefully.

Noise is unavoidable in any communication system, and it is commonly modeled using the AWGN channel, where random noise is added to the signal. Because of this, signals must be designed in a way that allows them to be recovered accurately. Techniques like bipolar signaling improve noise resistance, and matched filters are used at the receiver to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio at the sampling point. These ideas are critical for reducing errors during transmission.

Finally, the probability of error shows how signal quality directly affects reliability. Using metrics like Eb/N0, we can see that even small improvements in signal strength can drastically reduce error rates, a behavior known as the waterfall effect. At the same time, increasing data rate or using more complex signaling schemes requires higher signal quality. This highlights the core tradeoff of the physical layer, where power, bandwidth, data rate, and reliability are all interconnected.
